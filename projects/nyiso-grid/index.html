What the grid does when every building goes electric

Measuring how hard a three-day temperature drop pushed electricity demand in each of the eleven NYISO zones, as groundwork for the harder question of what happens to that relationship once building heat moves onto the grid.

Eleven zones, one cold snap

Between 3 and 5 February 2023 temperature across New York State fell sharply. Electricity demand climbed with it, but not by the same amount everywhere, and the differences turn out to be large.

The fitted sensitivity spans more than two orders of magnitude, from 52.7 MW per °F in New York City down to 0.43 MW per °F in the North zone. Some of that is simply size: New York City carries 5,862 MW against Millwood's 386 MW. But size is not the whole story, and normalising each zone against its own baseline is what makes the chart above worth looking at.

Normalised, the ordering changes. Millwood climbs hardest, to about 144% of its baseline load at a 40 °F fall, and it also has the tightest fit of any zone at R² 0.68. New York City, the largest zone by far, has the weakest fit at R² 0.32: its demand is the least explained by temperature of any zone in the state, which is what you would expect from a load dominated by commercial and base demand rather than heating.

Where the model breaks

North is the clear failure. Its linear term is −0.43 MW per °F against a mean load of 734 MW, which is a response of roughly six hundredths of a percent per degree, indistinguishable from none. With nothing for the linear term to do, the quadratic term takes over and the fitted curve bends downward, predicting that demand falls as the temperature does. That is not a physical result. It is a quadratic being fitted to a zone that has almost no temperature response to fit, and it is the clearest sign in the set that one functional form across eleven very different zones was the wrong choice.

West and Dunwoodie carry the same warning more quietly: both have positive squared terms, so their curves are convex where the other nine are concave.

What was built

The whole thing runs on three days of public market data and whatever weather stations happened to sit inside each zone.

Load data

NYISO real-time actual load for 3 to 5 February 2023: 10,109 zone-interval readings across the eleven zones at roughly five-minute resolution, 919 per zone, resampled to hourly.

Temperature

Hourly temperature for each zone, consolidated from several weather stations inside that zone and aligned to the load series by timestamp.

Model

Per zone, mean load grouped by temperature fall, then a least-squares fit of load on the fall and its square. Long Island was also fitted linearly on the binned means, at R² 0.436 and −12.04 MW per °F.

Diagnostics

Residual-against-temperature plots for all eleven zones, kept alongside each fit rather than reported only as a coefficient.

Output

Three plot families per zone covering mean load, the fitted regression and the residuals, and a fifteen-slide results deck.
